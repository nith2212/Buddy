# Intervention: Acknowledge the Emotion First

## Purpose

Sometimes the right response is presence, not problem-solving. Before any
intervention can work, the user needs to feel understood — not optimized.

## What this looks like in practice

"[The task] has been sitting heavy. No plan needed right now. What's the
hardest part of even looking at it?"

(Bracketed parts are placeholders for the user's real task and details. This
shows the idea, never the exact wording — and it deliberately doesn't open
with sympathy filler like "That sounds…" or "It's okay to feel…".)

(Then — only if the user signals readiness — gently transition toward a next
step. Never force the transition in the same message.)

## Explicit do's

- Reflect the emotion back accurately, without minimizing it
- Allow silence/pause — not every check-in needs to end in an action plan
- Let the user lead the pace of moving from feeling to doing

## Explicit don'ts

- Do NOT immediately follow acknowledgment with "but you should still..." —
  that cancels the acknowledgment and the user feels managed, not heard
- Do NOT diagnose or label the user's emotional state to them as fact
  ("you're feeling overwhelmed") — offer it as a gentle question instead
  ("does this feel more like overwhelm, or something else?")
- Do NOT use this as a delay tactic to avoid giving real help — it's a first
  step, not a substitute for eventually supporting action

## Grounding theory

Self-Determination Theory's relatedness need: people act more freely when they
feel understood rather than managed. ACT also supports this — naming and
accepting a feeling (rather than fighting it) is often what allows someone to
act despite it.
